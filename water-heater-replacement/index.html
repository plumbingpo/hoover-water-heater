How do you know it is time to replace a water heater rather than repair it? Age is the first clue. A tank past eight to twelve years has done most of its work, and the serial number on the label tells us exactly how old yours is. The symptoms come next. Water leaking from the tank, rust showing up only in the hot water, and heavy sediment noise all say the tank is close to finished. Parts like thermocouples, elements, and relief valves can be replaced on almost any heater, but a rusted-through tank cannot, and plenty of households in Hoover, AL usually find that out the hard way, with a wet floor.

A new water heater should fit the way your household uses hot water today, not the way it did when the old one went in. That is why Hoover Water Heater asks a few questions first: how many people shower in the morning, whether you fill a large tub, and how many bathrooms draw at once. Tank heaters are compared by first-hour rating, while tankless units are sized by gallons per minute at the temperature rise needed in the coldest months. Heat pump models get a check for space and airflow, since they pull heat from the room around them. We also confirm your gas line, vent, and electrical panel can handle the new unit, so there are no surprises on installation day.

The swap itself follows a checklist, not a guess: the old tank drained and hauled away, new connectors and a working shutoff installed, a sediment trap added and the gas line leak-tested, venting sized and pitched to the manufacturer's spec, and the relief valve piped to a safe discharge point. If local code requires a thermal expansion tank, a drain pan, or seismic straps, they go in, and the permit is pulled whenever one is needed. We finish by filling and purging the tank, checking draft and carbon monoxide on gas models, setting the thermostat near 120 degrees, and showing you around the new heater. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you money and keep a slow drip from turning into a flooded utility room. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Banging and popping from a tank packed with sediment
  • Rust or corrosion on the tank, fittings, or vent connector
  • A heater older than most tanks last, usually eight to twelve years
  • Operating costs climbing on an older, less efficient heater
  • Less and less hot water, even after repairs
  • A second or third repair within a year or two
  • Water leaking from the bottom or body of the tank
  • Rust-colored hot water while the cold stays clear

Simple Ways to Extend Your Water Heater's Life

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Here's where to begin:

  • Clean the air filter on a heat pump water heater every few months
  • Note the install date and keep up with maintenance so the next replacement is never an emergency
  • Avoid running the tank hotter than about 120 degrees unless there is a specific need
  • Have the anode rod checked around year three to five and replaced if it is worn
  • Descale a tankless unit every year, or more often with hard water
  • Flush the new tank once or twice a year to keep sediment from building up
  • Have water pressure checked; above about 80 psi, a pressure reducing valve protects the new heater
